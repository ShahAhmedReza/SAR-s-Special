# Creative Question: Work, Energy & Power

**[P1-Ch05: Work, Energy & Power | Micro-topic: Work (Definition, Formula, Sign Convention) | Tier 1 | Level: Knowledge(a)/Comprehension(b)/Application(c)/HigherOrder(d) — CQ]**

---

### Stem
A worker pulls a $20\text{ kg}$ crate along a rough horizontal floor using a rope inclined at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal, applying a force of $100\text{ N}$, causing a displacement of $5\text{ m}$. Later, the same worker pushes an identical crate with a force of $100\text{ N}$ directed at $120^\circ$ to the direction of displacement (i.e., partly opposing motion) over the same $5\text{ m}$. A student claims the work done in both cases is numerically equal in magnitude.

---

### Questions & Answers

#### (a) Define work in physics. (1 mark)

Work is said to be done when **a force acting on a body produces a displacement of the body in the direction of (or with a component along) the force**.

---

#### (b) Why is work a scalar quantity despite being defined using two vectors (force and displacement)? (2 marks)

Work is scalar because **it is defined as the dot product $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = F s \cos \theta$, and a dot product of two vectors always yields a quantity having magnitude only, no direction**.

Although both force and displacement are vector quantities possessing direction, the operation that combines them to define work — the scalar (dot) product — strips away directional character and returns only a magnitude with an algebraic sign (positive, negative, or zero). This sign indicates whether energy is added to or removed from the system, not a spatial direction, so work behaves and combines algebraically like a scalar.

---

#### (c) Calculate the work done by the worker while pulling the first crate. (3 marks)

The stem directly relates to **Work Done by a Constant Force at an Angle**.

> **Work Done by a Constant Force:** When a constant force $F$ acts on a body at an angle $\theta$ to its displacement $s$, the work done is $W = F s \cos \theta$, where only the component of force along the displacement ($F \cos \theta$) contributes to work.

From the stem:
* Force, $F = 100\text{ N}$
* Displacement, $s = 5\text{ m}$
* Angle, $\theta = 30^\circ$

$$W = F s \cos \theta = (100)(5) \cos 30^\circ = 500 \times 0.8660 = 433.0\text{ J}$$

Therefore, the work done by the worker while pulling the first crate is **$433.0\text{ J}$**, found using **Work Done by a Constant Force at an Angle**.

---

#### (d) Evaluate the student's claim by comparing the work done in both scenarios. (4 marks)

The student's claim is **incorrect — the two work values are not equal in magnitude; the second case gives $-250\text{ J}$, not $433\text{ J}$**.

> **Sign Convention in Work:**
> * When $0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ$, $\cos\theta > 0$, giving **positive work** (energy added).
> * When $\theta = 90^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0$, giving **zero work**.
> * When $90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ$, $\cos\theta < 0$, giving **negative work** (energy removed).

From the stem — second case:
* Force, $F = 100\text{ N}$
* Displacement, $s = 5\text{ m}$
* Angle, $\theta = 120^\circ$

$$W_2 = F s \cos \theta = (100)(5) \cos 120^\circ = 500 \times (-0.5) = -250\text{ J}$$

**Comparison:**
* $W_1 = +433.0\text{ J}$ (positive work, energy given to the crate)
* $W_2 = -250.0\text{ J}$ (negative work, energy removed/opposing motion)

The magnitudes ($433.0\text{ J}$ vs. $250.0\text{ J}$) are unequal, and the signs are opposite in physical meaning.

Therefore, **the two work values differ both in magnitude ($433.0\text{ J}$ vs. $250\text{ J}$) and in sign, so the student's claim of numerical equality is false**.
